World mythology encompasses the vast and diverse body of stories, deities, and
supernatural beings that ancient and modern cultures have used to explain the
universe, human existence, and moral values. While mythologies vary widely, they
share common themes, such as creation myths, hero’s journeys, and tales of gods
and monsters. These stories not only reflect cultural values but also serve as
historical records, shaping literature, religion, and philosophy across
civilizations.

In Mesopotamian mythology, some of the earliest recorded myths include the Epic
of Gilgamesh, which explores mortality, friendship, and divine wrath. The
Sumerians and Babylonians envisioned a pantheon of gods governing all aspects of
life, with figures like Enlil, Ishtar, and Marduk shaping their cosmology.
Similarly, Egyptian mythology revolved around gods like Osiris, Isis, and Ra,
with a strong focus on the afterlife and cosmic balance (Ma’at).

Greek and Roman myths, deeply influential in Western storytelling, feature an
extensive pantheon led by Zeus and Jupiter, respectively. Their myths emphasize
human-like deities with flaws, engaging in dramatic conflicts that mirror human
struggles. The hero’s journey, seen in Odysseus, Heracles, and Aeneas, became a
foundational narrative structure. Norse mythology, with its gods like Odin,
Thor, and Loki, portrays a harsh, fate-driven world culminating in Ragnarök, the
end and rebirth of existence.

Asian mythology includes the rich spiritual traditions of Hindu, Buddhist, and
Taoist stories. Hindu epics like the Ramayana and Mahabharata blend mythology
with religious teachings, featuring gods like Vishnu, Shiva, and Devi. Chinese
mythology often merges folklore with philosophy, as seen in the tales of the
Monkey King, the Eight Immortals, and cosmic forces like the Yin-Yang. Japanese
Shinto myths center on kami (spirits) and figures like Amaterasu, the sun
goddess, emphasizing nature and ancestry.

Indigenous mythologies across Africa, the Americas, and Oceania provide unique
perspectives on creation and human roles in the world. African myths often
feature trickster gods like Anansi and moral lessons tied to nature. Native
American myths vary by tribe but commonly depict Great Spirit creators, animal
guides, and cyclical views of time. In Polynesian mythology, figures like Maui
bring fire to humanity or pull islands from the sea, emphasizing themes of
ingenuity and survival.

Despite cultural differences, myths serve similar functions: they explain the
unknown, offer ethical lessons, and reinforce communal identity. In modern
times, mythology continues to inspire literature, film, and philosophy, proving
that these ancient narratives remain deeply embedded in the human experience.
